How I work

Four habits I don't skip

Users first

Every product I specify names the people it serves before it names a feature. The generic persona gets rejected every time.

Scope is the decision

A fourth feature, a soft metric, a product line we weren't ready to run. The things I cut are the clearest record of what I think the product is.

Attack before use

I'm trained in penetration testing as well as product. I work out how a product gets attacked before I work out how it gets used.

Own the trade-off

Every option on the table usually has a good argument. Picking one, explaining why, and owning it when it turns out wrong is the job.

About me

I build for the people most products forget

Most products are designed for an ideal user who doesn't exist. I'd rather start with the real one: the patient who can't take a day off to see a doctor, the freelancer juggling three apps to get paid, the customer who gives up halfway through sign-up.

I'm a product manager, and I don't limit myself to one industry. The job is the same wherever I work: find the real problem, decide who to serve first, cut what doesn't matter, and write it down clearly enough for designers and engineers to build.

Right now I do that at TagGrid, the company I cofounded, where I lead product from strategy through to the first release.

My cybersecurity training shapes how I work. Before anything ships, I want to know how it could be misused and who gets hurt if it fails. Whatever the product, that question belongs to the product team.

I like problems that don't fit a template, teams that argue for the user, and products that feel simple because someone did the hard thinking first.

    TagGrid

    A digital identity platform for Nigeria. Claim @yourname, and people can find you, verify you and pay you with it, in naira, dollars or crypto.

    Role
    Cofounder & CPO
    Timeframe
    2026 – present
    Status
    Pre-launch
    I owned
    Strategy, scope, compliance, partners

    Decisions that mattered

    • Identity is the product; payments are the way in.
    • Lead with one user, launch for two: freelancers paid in dollars, and everyday Nigerians.
    • Ship all three wallets together, or the address claim isn't real.
    • Build on licensed partners, and design so any of them can be swapped.
    • Write the compliance suite before the first user exists.

    Context

    TagGrid started as a 121-page document: every feature the product could ever have. Wallets, cards, bills, savings, investments, crypto, a merchant layer, an identity layer. All of it was reasonable. None of it was a product yet.

    I came in as the product cofounder with one job: turn that into something we could build, launch and defend.

    The problem

    Nigeria has more wallet apps than anyone can name, and they compete on the same thing: moving naira between banks slightly faster or slightly cheaper than the next one. Entering that fight with less money than the incumbents was not a strategy.

    But underneath the feature list was an idea nobody in the market owned. Nigerian money has no address. You receive money by reading a ten-digit account number aloud on WhatsApp. If you earn from abroad, you juggle a bank app, a dollar account and a crypto exchange, and move money between them by hand.

    Account numbers move money. Addresses build networks.

    What I decided

    Identity is the product. I sorted every feature into three layers and deferred anything that didn't make the Tag more useful to know.

    IntelligenceVerified income history, insight, merchant identity. What becomes possible once the first two layers have run for a while.
    Value exchangeNaira, dollar and crypto wallets, conversion, cards, bills, the ledger. The providers underneath are replaceable by design.
    IdentityThe Tag, KYC and KYB, the directory that maps a name to an account. Never outsourced. This is the company.

    Lead with one user, launch for two. The first user we design around is the Nigerian freelancer or remote worker earning in dollars and living in naira. They feel the pain most sharply, and every foreign client who pays them sees the product work. Everyday Nigerians who send money and pay bills come in from launch too.

    Ship the wallets together. The obvious lean move was one wallet first. I argued the opposite. If a Tag only receives naira, it's just another account number with a nickname. The claim only holds if the same address takes naira, dollars and crypto.

    Build on partners, and design to switch. Custody, transfers and cards run through licensed financial institutions. I compared providers on their fees rather than their feature lists, and kept the Tag-to-account directory in our own database, so a provider can change without moving a single user.

    Compliance before launch. I wrote the AML/CFT policy, privacy policy, terms of use and risk disclosure before a single user existed, and built KYC and KYB into onboarding. A payments product is an attack surface, and I'd rather specify fraud controls up front than retrofit them after the first incident.

    What I produced

    • Product strategy and positioning
    • Vision and roadmap with gated milestones and a KPI framework
    • First-release scope
    • Sign-up, KYC and account recovery flows in Figma
    • Legal and compliance suite: AML/CFT, privacy, terms of use, risk disclosure, acceptable use
    • Financial and accounting system specification
    • Partner evaluation and due diligence framework

    Where it stands

    Pre-launch. The company is incorporated and the first release is scoped. The first metric that matters, how many users receive money to a Tag more than once, doesn't exist yet, and I haven't invented targets before we have a baseline.

    What I'd do differently

    I spent too long on breadth before forcing the first-user decision. The three-layer model was useful, but it was also a way of keeping every feature alive under a tidier heading. Choosing who to build for first is what moved the product, and I should have made that call sooner.

    Abara Care

    The chronic-care feature of a Nigerian telemedicine app: research, a PRD and a working prototype, built to turn a one-off consultation into a doctor who follows up.

    Type
    Product study + prototype
    Year
    2026
    Output
    Research, PRD, prototype, build log
    Prototype
    Try it ↗

    Decisions that mattered

    • Re-diagnose it: retention was a continuity problem.
    • Build for Mama Chidinma, 52, a market trader in Enugu with high blood pressure.
    • Three features as one loop, and cut the fourth.
    • Smart features support the relationship; a licensed doctor makes every clinical decision.
    • Measure whether people come back, with a guardrail against spammy nudges.

    Context

    The brief described Abara, a Nigerian telemedicine app whose founder wanted it to feel like "a family doctor, not an emergency room," and whose 30-day retention sat at 14 percent. I researched the problem, wrote the PRD and built a working prototype.

    The problem

    Fourteen percent sounds like a broken product. It isn't. Health apps lose around seventy percent of their users within the first weeks, and general health apps keep about six percent at day thirty. Abara was above the benchmark, and people were leaving because the app stopped feeling relevant, not because of bugs.

    The Nigerian layer sharpened it. Out-of-pocket payments make up 70 to 77 percent of health spending, so people pay per crisis and seek care per crisis. And around 30 percent of Nigerian adults have hypertension. Fewer than 40 percent know it, and fewer than 20 percent have it under control.

    Abara's retention problem was a continuity problem.

    What I decided

    Fix continuity. Every competitor optimises the single consultation. Nobody owns what happens after. Abara already had verified doctors, a patient-owned record and a family plan, and was using them one visit at a time.

    Build for Mama Chidinma. The obvious persona was a 35-year-old urban professional. The research pointed elsewhere: a 52-year-old who sells provisions at Ogbete Main Market in Enugu, pays cash, and can't close her stall for half a day to queue at a hospital. She doesn't need another way to reach a doctor in an emergency. She needs a doctor who keeps an eye on her between them.

    My care planThe reason to return: what to track, how often, the target, the medication.
    Check-ins and nudgesPaced reminders that escalate to her doctor when readings drift.
    Same doctorShe never starts from zero. Every conversation opens with her history summarised.

    Three features, one loop. A fourth, a health-tips feed, was on the table. I cut it. Content doesn't fix a relevance problem.

    A licensed doctor decides. The app's smart features only summarise readings, time the nudges, flag values outside the doctor's range and draft a summary. A licensed Nigerian doctor makes every clinical decision, and every screen says so.

    Measure whether people come back. I replaced daily active users with 30-day retention (14 to 30-plus percent in two quarters), care-plan return rate and repeat-doctor rate, plus a guardrail: nudge mute rate under 10 percent.

    Building it

    I built the prototype in Lovable. It only became reliable once I specified the behaviour and data exactly: six seeded readings from 156/96 down to 132/82, the thresholds for each status colour, and one shared data set so logging a reading updates every screen at once. The patient is fictional, and nothing is stored or sent.

    What I'd do differently

    I added the privacy constraints after the first build instead of writing them in from the start. On a health product, safety belongs in the first spec. And the weakest point in the loop is manual entry. A cheap cuff that syncs a reading in one tap is the first thing I'd build next.

    Pennywise

    A personal finance coach for young Nigerian professionals, taken from problem to PRD.

    Type
    Product study
    Year
    2026
    Output
    Market brief, PRD
    Market
    Nigeria

    Decisions that mattered

    • Rebuild the brief for Nigeria instead of localising a US one.
    • Sit above the savings and banking apps rather than compete with them.
    • Draw the line between coaching and regulated advice, and write it down.
    • Scope the MVP to two things: where the money went, then one insight.
    • Drop the soft metric and add a safety metric.

    Context

    A product study in two parts: a market brief, then a PRD built on top of it. I picked personal finance because it's where a Western template fails a Nigerian user most completely.

    The problem

    Nigeria's inflation hit 34.8 percent at the end of 2024, and the naira lost more than half its value in two years. According to EFInA, 84 percent of Nigerian adults ran out of money in the previous twelve months.

    The apps young Nigerians already use, PiggyVest, Cowrywise, Kuda, are good at holding money. None of them coach. None of them ask why you keep hitting zero on the 22nd.

    Nothing had looked at her actual situation and told her what to do, in order.

    What I decided

    Rebuild the brief for Nigeria. The first pass leaned on American statistics. Accurate, and useless. I rebuilt it from EFInA survey data, CBN inflation figures, and the apps Nigerians actually have on their phones.

    PennywiseThe coaching layer. Reads across every account and tells you what to do next, in English or Pidgin, on WhatsApp if that's where you live.
    PiggyVestHolds savings
    CowrywiseHolds investments
    KudaHolds the account

    Sit above, don't compete. Pennywise connects to the apps people already use and reads across them. It doesn't need to win a fight it can't win.

    Coaching, not advice. SEC Nigeria regulates who can advise on securities. A coach that drifts into "buy this" is a licensing problem before it's a product problem, so the brief draws the line explicitly.

    A persona built from real numbers. Amaka earns ₦195,000 a month at a Lagos agency, sends ₦20,000 to her mother in Enugu, pays ₦55,000 rent and ₦15,000 a month on a loan. Her problem isn't discipline. Every user story had to work for her.

    Scope the MVP to two things. Show where the money went, with categories that reflect Nigerian life (rent, family transfers, loan repayments), then surface the one change that matters most this month. Irregular-income budgeting moved out of the MVP because it needs ninety days of data.

    Drop the soft metric, add the safety one. Self-reported financial confidence was on the list. I replaced it with behaviour change tracked in real transactions, then added a safety metric: any conversation flagged for human review gets a response within 24 hours, every time.
